03 · Before Pixnub

Planning the shoot for Pixnub

Most people shoot, then look for a template that fits. Turn that around.

1Start from your creative

List the products one job sells. Every one has a slot the athlete has to fit. Then grid your poses against them.

  • For this dancer: the side lean feeds the memory mate. Hat in front feeds the portrait. The leap only goes on designs built for a silhouette.
  • The team pose is also a portrait. Don't filter it out of your portraits.
  • An empty square means you don't sell that product from that pose, or you build a template for it. Decide the gaps before the shoot.

2One pose order per sport

Shoot the same order every time and each pose has a known number. Pose two is the same pose for every athlete.

  • Give every pose a plain name. Keep a laminated pose card at every station, one per sport.
  • The pose number is a first guess. A person confirms it, and a pose that is each athlete's own gets sorted by eye.
  • Pose one, the team pose, earns twice. It builds the team photo and sells as a portrait.

4Setting up the shoot

Same distance, every frame. Every station, every day, makeup days included. Then a cutout's height is the athlete's real height.

Tap the name, then shoot. Entagged on a tablet, fed by the names list in GotPhoto, writes that athlete's code into every frame. The classic mistake: shooting before the tap.

5Notes on the day

One printed sheet, one team per page, a column per pose plus buddies and glare. The crew writes frame numbers on it.

The notes become buddy tags, retouch flags and the makeup day list. With no note, someone looks at every frame again.

6One folder per pose

Sort the cutouts into one folder per pose, named with the number and the pose: P1 Standing front. Shot order is the first guess. A person confirms.

The folder is the only tag a frame needs. Limit each template to the pose folders it runs on (page 8).

04 · Targeting modes

Every targeting mode on one chart

A mode is the rule for placing the athlete in the slot. You choose it once, when you set up the template. Day to day you will live in 3, 11 and 13.

Face modes1 to 6Find the face and work from red guide lines. For cutouts with a clear face.
1

Sizes the athlete by the face. It doesn't care where the feet are.

Use it for knee-up and thigh-up shots.

You draw one red box.

2

Centers on the face. Stands the feet on your line.

Use it for full body.

You draw a cross and a bottom line.

3

Top of the athlete to one line, bottom to the other, centered on the face.

Use it for portraits. The default.

You draw a top line, a bottom line and a face line.

4

Like 3, but centered on the body's outline. Pixnub offers it for a back or a mask. We use the box modes.

5 and 6

Top of the athlete on a line. Sized by the face. 5 centers on the face, 6 on the body's outline.

Use it for shots that aren't full body.

Drop-in modes7 to 10Place a whole photo that isn't cut out.
7

Fit the canvas. The whole photo shows. Leaves empty space.

8

Fill the canvas. No empty space. Crops the edges.

9

Fit a frame. The whole photo, inside a frame you draw.

10

Fill a frame. The frame is filled. Crops the edges.

Day to day

One shape of photo, another shape of frame, and you want it filled. That is mode 10.

Box modes11 to 13Ignore the face and fit the cutout into a green box you set.
11

Pins the top of the cutout to the box.

12

Centers the cutout in the box.

13

Stands the cutout on the bottom of the box.

Use the box for

A back to the camera, a mask, a silhouette, buddy photos. The cutout is scaled up or down until it just fits the box.

Do keep the whole box on the canvas. A box that runs past the edge acts smaller than it looks.

Don't let a box shrink an arms-up pose and call it done.

Mode 3: raised arms count as height, so an arms-up pose shrinks the whole athlete. The guides are drawn over real photos, to the published rule. They are not renders out of SPA.

Two templates, and your hands

For most studios, one face-mode template for normal standing poses and one box-mode template with a big enough box cover about 99 percent of frames. Place the rest by hand.

Hand placement is a good answer. Nothing says a studio has to be fully automated.

Try the face mode first

If the athlete comes out within ten percent of their size in the team pose and stays inside the safe area, keep it. If not, the box. If the box gets it wrong too, place it by hand.

No face to find? Give a helmet, a mask or a back a box-mode template in the CSV, before you render.

Top of the cutout to the top of the face.

mode 3
a box

The same kick. In mode 3 the raised leg counts as height and she comes out small. In a box she doesn't.

Find the hard poses before the render

Measure from the top of the cutout to the top of the face. Ten percent of the cutout's height or more and something is over the head: an arm, a leg, a prop. Those are the frames for your box template, or your hand pass.

Place the rest by hand

  1. 1
    Run what you know works.
  2. 2
    Put the frames you know won't into their own small CSV.
  3. 3
    Run those on a box-mode template with Use Smart Objects on. It is off for a normal run.
  4. 4
    Open each result and move the athlete into place.
  5. 5
    Save.

How we do it: more than one box

Later, if you want it. A box centers the cutout's outline, not the athlete's weight, so Lake Photography builds more than one box template and gives each kind of placement its own folder. On the demo job they are REGULAR, TALL, SHORT and SILHOUETTE.

It only pays with AI keeping the lists. Two templates and your hands cover the same poses.

Once it's placed: four small things make the athlete stand in the scene

Clip the adjustments

A Levels and a Hue and Saturation layer, clipped to the athlete's group so they only touch the athlete.

A glow behind

A soft white shape, about half see-through, between the background and the athlete. It separates them from the room.

Dust or fog in front

A layer in front that covers the feet. Feet that are covered look planted. Feet you can see the edge of look cut out.

Where the feet stand

Find the horizon in the artwork. The feet stand clearly below it, about a boot height.

Anatomy of a template

A template is a normal Photoshop file. What makes it a template is the names.

Its layers, top first. The guide can be hidden or showing: it makes no difference to the render. Art layers can be called anything. The names SPA reaches for cannot.

The player slot

SPAPixnub'sPlayer-1which athleteMode-3the mode
  • The mode is in the slot's name. SPA reads the guide that belongs to that slot.
  • Other slots: SPA-Player-2-Mode-1, SPA-Team-Mode-1, SPA-Logo-Mode-1.
  • Copy the slot from Pixnub's starter file, spa-smart-objects.psd, or let Setup Template add it. Don't build one from scratch.
  • After any edit, run Analyze.

Text layers

A text layer is named for the column that fills it. All caps, no spaces. No matching layer and nothing happens, with no error.

Layer nameFilled by
FIRSTNAME LASTNAMEThe athlete's name columns
NUMBERThe number column
SPATEXT1 to SPATEXT5Five spare text columns: the club, the number with a hash, the year, a sponsor

Decide once what each holds, the same in every template. Ours: 1 the club, 2 the number, 3 the year.

Text that fits: -R and -RM

LASTNAME-R

Every name is resized to the placeholder's width, up or down.

LASTNAME-RM

Only names that are too long shrink. Use this almost everywhere.

Three things decide how a name lands

  • The sample text. Its width is the limit. Leave long made-up text as the placeholder, never a real short name.
  • The anchor. Left, center or right is the side it shrinks toward.
  • The ending. Safest setup: right-aligned, long sample, -RM.

Before a job

  • Find the longest and the shortest name on the names list and run those two. If both look good, everything between them will.
  • If the design is in capitals, switch capitals on as a text style. Don't type it that way.

Show and hide

  • A row can show up to five layers and hide up to five, by exact name.
  • Keep those names unique in the file.
  • SPA turns on every text layer it fills. To hide text, hide the group around it, never the text layer.

Naming the file

OUTLIERdesign family45VproductM3mode.psd

Read the name and you know what it is and how it places. Product codes: 45V portrait, 45TV name portrait, MM memory mate, BTN button.

Where templates live

  • One flat folder of templates per job. SPA won't look in subfolders.
  • Keep the master and one past version. Never save a rendered output over a master. When a template changes, test it again before the full run.

09 · Running a job

Check, run, count, then look

A running batch owns Photoshop. Check the CSV, test it, then keep your hands off.

The machine: five checks

Photoshop open, with no documents open.
Action sets loaded, one copy of each.
Facepoints running and showing connected. If it doesn't say connected, don't press Run.
Fonts installed.
Scratch disk on a drive that isn't your startup drive.

While it runs

No copy and paste. No opening a file to check something. To stop it, hold Escape. Reload the CSV in SPA after any change to it.

The test run comes first

The test proves coverage, not count. Every template, every team, every odd pose, once. It is done when every template and team pair has a product you looked at. Its output goes to its own folder, never in with the finals. When a template changes, test it again.

After the run: two counts, three looks

Brain Check says missing 0. The file that ran, against the folder it rendered into.
No file name ends in _2. SPA never overwrites. A _2 means an old file was in the way.
One contact sheet per template. 179 of 179 memory mates carried another club's team photo. Every file was there.
One product per team color, opened. 663 memory mates had no team color. Every file was there.
The longest name and the shortest, opened. 1,407 images rerun. The text placeholder was narrower than the names.

Copy SPA's failed list out first. A fresh batch wipes it. A count says the files exist. Only your eyes say what's in them.

Big jobs, crashes and reruns

  • Smart objects off. Restart Photoshop between chunks of about four thousand rows.
  • After a crash, nothing that rendered is lost. Relaunch Photoshop, then run one rerun file of only the rows with no finished file.
  • A rerun is as big as the problem. One name fix is a handful of rows, lifted from the file that actually ran.
  • Move the old files out first. SPA never overwrites: a file already there makes the new one _2.

When something is wrong, debug in this order

1Which file ran?
2What do its cells say?
3What does the template hold?
4What does the action do?

Getting any designer's file ready

A designer sells you a look, built for someone editing one athlete by hand. Pixnub needs it set up for a machine. Don't learn each designer's names. Give every file yours.

Three things reach into your template by name

The contract: the same names in every template

Decide your names once. Then every template you own, from any designer, carries exactly those names. One CSV fills all of them, one color switch works on all of them, and one action runs on all of them.

Every template carries every name on your list, even where the design doesn't use it. A missing group stops a batch three hundred images in. A missing text layer or switch is quieter and just as bad: nothing happens, and nothing tells you.

The names are for Pixnub. The order is for you. Where the design needs a different stacking to look right, the design wins.

Color, kept simple

  • Color should live in a few named places. If recoloring takes twenty layers, get the count down.
  • The cleanest build: art in white, gray and black, with one color layer above it.
  • Never pick a color off a lit photo. Use the club's real color value.

The prep, in seven steps

  1. 1
    Copy it. The purchased original is never edited.
  2. 2
    Clear out. Guides off, pick one of each option, park the rest in 00 OFF.
  3. 3
    Resize to your build size and check it's sRGB.
  4. 4
    Add the player slot from Pixnub's starter file and choose its mode.
  5. 5
    Rename to your list. Fix the typos you inherited.
  6. 6
    Add any name on your list the design is missing. Sort into the six groups and color-label them.
  7. 7
    Test. Three poses that place very differently: standing, a high kick, a leap. Every switch once, every action once. Then list the layer names and check them against your list.

Checking the names, three ways. By hand, compare a screenshot of the Layers panel after any save. In Pixnub, Analyze reports which layers it found. With an AI assistant, list every layer name and flag anything missing, repeated or misspelled.

12 · Teams

Building teams in CAT

No team photo is shot on the day. One chosen frame per athlete goes into a folder, and CAT arranges them.

The panel, in the order you set it

1
Source Folder

One frame per athlete, the same pose for everyone. Ours is pose one, full body. CAT builds as soon as you pick the folder.

2
Canvas Settings

The size of the team photo. Ours is 10 x 8 in at 300 PPI.

3
Team Placement

The box the team has to fit in. Ours is 8.5 x 6.5 in, 0.75 in from the top and the left. CAT centers the team in it, stands it on the bottom edge and picks one size for the whole team. Size the box to where the team sits in your template.

4
Build Settings

Ours: Team Vertical Alignment on Bottom, 3D Perspective on at 75 mm, Player Spacing 75, Row Spacing 30, Player to Player Shadows on (one light). Match Scaling by Face Size off: it can make a taller athlete come out shorter. True height needs every athlete shot from the same distance.

5
Save New Preset

So the settings stick, and the next team with this layout starts here.

6
Save Build

So this team can be opened again. Ours go in TEAM BUILDS, beside the source folder.

7
Save .JPG

Last, because this is the file the memory mates use.

A dance routine's team photo. Nobody stood together: it is built from individual cutouts.

The craft pass

The app gets you most of the way. Then a person looks.

  • Nothing across a face.
  • No feet on top of feet.
  • Every athlete's upper body showing.
  • Head count against the names list.

Enter Adjust Mode moves one athlete. Get it right before the run: change one team photo and every memory mate on that team renders again.

Preset or build

The preset holds settings: canvas, team box, build settings. Nothing about who is in the picture. Save one per layout.

The build is this team, a .catb file, and it points at its source folder. Move the folder and the build will not open.

The name the memory mate looks for

Every memory mate row has a TEAM FILE cell. SPA takes the name from that cell and looks for that file in its Team Images Folder.

Intermediate.jpg in the cell, Intermediate.jpg on the drive. Same spelling, same extension. If SPA Factory wrote the cell from the group name, save under the group name.

The makeup athlete

An athlete who missed the day gets shot later and added. This is why you saved the build.

  • Open Build, then Add Player.
  • Save under the same name, so the new file replaces the old one.
  • Rerun only the rows whose TEAM FILE is that file.

Rows

Short in front, tall in back. Tallest in the middle of each row. Faces in the gaps of the row ahead. Balanced rows, never one athlete tacked on the end.

Coaches and goalies

Coaches in the back row. Goalies on the ends of the front row. The settings to look at are Forced Top and Forced Bottom, in Build Settings.

The floor line

CAT stands each cutout on its lowest point. A stick or a prop below the feet makes that athlete stand too high. Look along the feet before you save.

Setting up an AI assistant

Forget the hype. For this work an assistant is a checker with a rules page. It reads what you give it, checks it against your rules, and tells you what it found. You still decide.

1A folder

The same job folder you already built. Good folders make this easy.

2One page of rules

Short, plain sentences it reads every time. The first time it gets something wrong, add a line.

3A real sample

Your real header row and one full row, so it never has to guess what your files look like.

Say one sentence every time you ask, or make it a rule: "Show me the row count and one full row before you tell me anything else." Never accept "all good." If it can't show you what it read, don't trust what it says.

A routine card is a recipe card

You don't explain the job in a chat each time. You write it down once, like a recipe card, and the assistant follows the card. We call these routine cards. Five lines.

The fourth line is the one people leave off, and it's the one that makes it right.

Once the card exists, what you type is short: "Count the render for this job." The detail lives on the card, where you can fix it once. Typed the same long explanation twice? That is a card you haven't written.

Three kinds of assistant. A chat window is enough to start. One that can open your files can count a folder against a CSV. And Pixnub's forum has one that answers around the clock.

Made-up names

When you're asking how something works, use made-up names. Real athlete data only goes into an account your studio pays for and controls. Real codes never go into anything you share.

Have it write the tool once

A chat is fine to start. But a chat that rebuilds your names list fresh every job is a new set of mistakes every job. Once a job repeats, have it write the tool once. Save it. Run it.

Training it, and what to do on Monday

Training it to get it right

  1. 1
    Show it a right one. A CSV that ran clean. A roster you already fixed. Tell it, this is what right looks like.
  2. 2
    Test it on work you've already checked. Check fifty to a hundred yourself, run it without showing it your answers, and compare. You find out what it misses before a parent does.
  3. 3
    Every miss becomes a line. Don't just correct it. Write down why, on the card or on the rules page, so it's right next time and every time after.

A good assistant tells you what it isn't sure of. A list marked "a person should look" is the point, not a failure.

This is the training. A rules page is nothing but these lines.

On Monday, pick one

It's the easiest to check, and it pays off the first time a batch dies.
